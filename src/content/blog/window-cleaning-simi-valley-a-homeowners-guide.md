## Why Simi Valley Glass Gets Dirty Differently

Simi Valley is a long east-west valley with the Santa Susana Mountains on its north side and the Simi Hills to the south. That shape, plus distance from the ocean, produces three soiling patterns coastal cities do not see together:

- **Santa Ana dust.** In fall and winter, dry offshore winds push fine dust across the valley in a day or two. Because there is little marine layer to dampen it, the dust lands dry and stays.
- **Wildfire ash.** The brush-covered hills around the valley have burned repeatedly, including the 2003 Simi Fire and the 2018 Woolsey Fire, which started in the Simi Hills near the Santa Susana Field Laboratory. Ash is gritty and alkaline when wet, so it needs a full rinse rather than a wipe.
- **Sprinkler overspray in summer heat.** Inland summers dry overspray on glass within minutes, which leaves every mineral the water carried as a white spot.

The table shows how each one behaves on glass and what we do about it.

| Soiling source | When it peaks | What it does on glass | How we handle it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Santa Ana dust | Fall and winter wind events | Even gray film, heaviest on the windward side | Pre-rinse, agitate, purified-water final rinse |
| Wildfire ash | Late summer to winter | Gritty deposit that scratches if wiped dry | Soak and rinse first, then wash; screens rinsed separately |
| Sprinkler spots | Summer irrigation season | White mineral rings where overspray dries | Wash, then purified-water rinse so no new spots form |
| Pollen and road dust | Spring, plus homes near the 118 | Sticky yellow-gray film on sills and screens | Screen and sill cleaning in the same visit |

## Simi Valley Water and Why the Rinse Matters

Like most Southern California tap water, Simi Valley's carries enough dissolved minerals to spot glass when it dries. The exact hardness depends on which utility serves your street, so check that utility's annual water quality report rather than a water softener ad, which will usually quote a scarier number.

For windows the more useful number is the rinse. The National Glass Association and International Window Cleaning Association's joint standard for cleaning architectural glass says pure rinse water should be **20 ppm total dissolved solids or less**, and that "the use of tap water is not acceptable" ([NGA/IWCA Glass Technical Paper FB01-00](https://www.glass.org/sites/default/files/2023-12/FB01-00_2023_Proper_Procedures_Cleaning_Architectural_Glass_Products.pdf)). [Unger](https://usa.ungerglobal.com/blog/10-common-mistakes-in-pure-water-window-cleaning/), which makes pure-water cleaning systems, says water above **10 ppm** may not give a spot-free finish. That is why we never finish a Simi Valley job with hose water, and why hosing your windows after a windstorm usually trades dust for spots.
