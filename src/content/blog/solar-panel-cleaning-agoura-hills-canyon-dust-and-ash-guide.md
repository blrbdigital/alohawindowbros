## Why Agoura Hills Solar Panels Soil Faster Than Average

Agoura Hills sits in a wind-funneled pocket of the Santa Monica Mountains where canyon corridors, chaparral hillsides, and the 101 freeway combine to keep airborne particulate moving across rooftops year-round.

> "Wildfire ash is one of the most damaging forms of solar soiling because it is alkaline and slightly sticky. When ash settles on warm panel glass and is then touched by morning dew or marine moisture, it cements into a bonded crust that ordinary rain cannot remove."

The major contributors to soiling on Agoura Hills solar panels are:

- **Canyon and chaparral dust** — fine mineral particulate carried on canyon winds settles on panels continuously through the dry season
- **Wildfire ash** — Agoura Hills sat in the heart of the 2018 Woolsey Fire burn zone, and ash from regional fires routinely drifts onto local rooftops
- **Santa Ana wind events** — strong offshore winds blow accumulated dust and ash onto panels in heavy waves several times a year
- **Oak and chaparral pollen** — coast live oak and native shrubs shed fine pollen from spring through early summer
- **Marine-layer moisture** — overnight humidity from the nearby coast dampens dust and ash so it sticks to glass instead of blowing off

According to the [National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL)](https://www.nrel.gov/), soiling losses climb fastest in dry, dusty climates with infrequent rain — exactly the conditions Agoura Hills faces for most of the year.

## Why Wildfire Ash Is the Biggest Threat to Agoura Hills Panels

Most homeowners think of dust as the main enemy. In Agoura Hills, ash is worse. Ash particles are alkaline, abrasive, and far stickier than dry dust once moisture touches them.

> "After a major wildfire, solar production can drop 15 to 25 percent within days as ash settles across panels. Because ash is alkaline and bonds chemically to glass when wet, every week it sits on a panel makes it harder to remove and increases the risk of permanent staining."

This is also why DIY ash removal backfires. Wiping dry ash with a cloth grinds abrasive particles across the anti-reflective coating, and rinsing with a garden hose spreads hard-water minerals on top of the ash. Every reputable solar manufacturer specifies deionized or reverse-osmosis water and soft, non-abrasive brushes for exactly this reason.

## When to Schedule Solar Panel Cleaning in Agoura Hills

Timing affects how much energy your cleaning recovers.

- **Early spring (March–April)** — clean before peak production season; the highest-impact cleaning of the year
- **Early summer (June)** — remove the pollen-and-dust film that builds through late spring
- **Late fall (October–November)** — clear dry-season dust before winter rains cement residue onto the glass
- **Immediately after any regional wildfire** — ash bonds harder the longer it sits, so prompt removal protects both output and the glass coating
- **After Santa Ana wind events** — strong winds blow canyon dust and ash onto rooftops in waves
- **Anytime production drops more than 10%** — a sustained, weather-unexplained decline is most often soiling

> "Rain removes only about half of solar soiling losses in dry chaparral climates, because bonded ash and pollen films resist water alone. Agoura Hills's long dry season means panels run through the entire peak-production period with no effective natural washing."

### Does wildfire ash damage solar panels?

Wildfire ash is alkaline and slightly abrasive, and it bonds chemically to panel glass when it gets damp from marine moisture or dew. Left in place, it can drop production 15 to 25 percent and risks permanently staining the anti-reflective coating. Prompt removal with purified water and soft brushes protects both output and the glass. Wiping dry ash with a cloth grinds abrasive particles across the coating and should be avoided.
