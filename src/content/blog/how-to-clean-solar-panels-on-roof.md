**To clean solar panels on a roof safely, work from the ground whenever you can: wait until the panels are cool in the early morning, rinse loose grit off with low-pressure water, brush gently with a soft solar-rated brush on a water-fed pole, and finish with purified or deionized water so minerals do not dry onto the glass.** Never pressure wash, never stand or kneel on the panels, and never climb in wind. Before any soap touches the glass, open the installation manual for your panels, because the manufacturers disagree: REC allows a mild biodegradable washing-up liquid, while Qcells prohibits surfactants, the cleaning agents in dish soap.

This guide is written by Aloha Window Bros, a two-brother window and solar panel cleaning team serving Ventura County and Santa Barbara County. It puts what three panel manufacturers and one cleaning-equipment maker actually publish side by side, so you can match the method to the panels on your roof instead of to a generic video.

> **Key takeaways**
> - **Cool, low, gentle, pure.** Clean cool panels, from the ground, with low-pressure water, a soft brush, and a purified final rinse.
> - **Your panel manual decides the soap question.** REC permits a mild biodegradable washing-up liquid. Qcells says no surfactants. First Solar allows only a mild detergent between pH 6.5 and 8.5.
> - **Pressure washers are out.** REC and Qcells both prohibit high-pressure cleaning, and First Solar caps nozzle pressure at 500 psi.
> - **Switching off the inverter does not make the panels safe to touch.** First Solar warns that daylight voltage in an array is enough to cause a lethal shock.
> - **Most California roofs do not need frequent washing.** A UC San Diego study of 186 California sites measured a 7.4% loss after a 145-day dry spell. Bird droppings, low-tilt arrays, and nearby dust sources are the exceptions.
> - **If the array cannot be reached from the ground, hire it out.** Every source below treats roof access, not scrubbing, as the real risk.

## Why Roof Solar Panels Get Dirty in the First Place

A coastal homeowner in Ventura may see several kinds of contamination on the same array. Dust carried inland during a dry wind event settles into the panel texture, pollen adds a fine film, and salt aerosols bond to the glass during humid coastal cycles. If birds nest on the roof, droppings collect near the upper edges and dry into stubborn patches. A nearby sprinkler adds another problem: mineral spots that get harder to remove as the glass heats and dries.

The lower edge usually tells the story. Rain and rinse water move dirt downhill, where it gathers against the frame and dries in a concentrated strip. Low-tilt installations hold more of that residue because water and debris do not run off as quickly as they do on steeper arrays. Many Southern California tract homes have modest roof pitches, so their panels may need more attention than a steeply mounted system in an open location.

<a id="surface-film-versus-functional-contamination"></a>
### Surface film versus functional contamination

Not every dull-looking panel needs washing. A light cosmetic film may have little practical effect, while an opaque bird deposit, thick dust band, salt crust, or baked mineral spot blocks light where it sits. The difference matters because aggressive cleaning creates its own risk. You should not climb onto a roof to remove a faint haze if the array is still producing normally.

Look for patterns rather than judging from one angle:

- **Lower-edge buildup:** A distinct dirty band along the frame means repeated runoff and drying.
- **Bird deposits:** White or dark opaque spots deserve targeted soaking, not hard scrubbing.
- **Salt haze:** Coastal film looks cloudy rather than gritty and can return quickly after cleaning.
- **Sprinkler spotting:** Circular mineral marks point to irrigation overspray, not ordinary dust.

<a id="how-much-soiling-actually-costs-you"></a>
## How Much Soiling Actually Costs You

**Soiling is the loss of solar output caused by dust, pollen, salt, bird droppings, and other deposits that block sunlight from reaching the cells.** It has a measurable cost, but the size of that cost depends heavily on climate and rainfall.

Worldwide, the [IEA PVPS executive summary on soiling losses](https://iea-pvps.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/IEA-PVPS-T13-21-2022-EXEC-SUMM-Soiling-Losses-PV-Plants.pdf) estimates that soiling cost at least **3% to 4% of annual PV energy production in 2018**, an economic loss of roughly **3 to 5 billion euros**, and expected that to reach **4% to 5% and 4 to 7 billion euros by 2023**. The [National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL, now the National Laboratory of the Rockies)](https://www.nlr.gov/news/detail/features/2021/scientists-studying-solar-try-solving-a-dusty-problem) puts annual soiling losses at **as much as 7% in parts of the United States** and as high as **50% in the Middle East**.

For California homes, the most useful number comes from the ground. [Engineers at UC San Diego](https://today.ucsd.edu/story/cleaning_solar_panels_often_not_worth_the_cost_engineers_at_uc_san_diego_fi) analyzed output from **186 residential and commercial sites** between the Bay Area and the Mexican border. Panels that went **145 days without rain or cleaning** during a summer drought lost only **7.4%** of their efficiency, a little under **0.05% per day**. For a typical 5 kW home system, washing halfway through that summer would have gained about **$20** of electricity.

| Setting | Soiling loss | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Worldwide PV fleet, 2018 | At least 3% to 4% of annual energy | IEA PVPS |
| Parts of the United States | Up to 7% a year | NREL |
| California homes, 145 dry days | 7.4% total, under 0.05% a day | UC San Diego |
| Middle East | Up to 50% a year | NREL |

The same UC San Diego study named the exceptions, and they are the cases where rooftop cleaning earns its keep:

- **Bird droppings** block essentially all light where they sit and do not wash away in rain.
- **Low tilt:** panels mounted at less than 5 degrees lost more, because dirt does not slide off.
- **Local dust sources:** homes directly next to and downwind of a highway, factory, or farm field can collect enough dirt to justify cleaning.

Coastal salt film and wildfire ash behave like the bird-dropping case more than the dust case: rain moves them around without removing them. Our [solar panel cleaning value guide](/blog/is-solar-panel-cleaning-worth-it/) walks through the cost math, but the practical rule is simple. Compare recent production with a similar-weather period, then look at the glass. A drop plus a visible deposit is a reason to clean. A dull look with normal output usually is not.

<a id="what-panel-manufacturers-actually-allow"></a>
## What Panel Manufacturers Actually Allow

Most online cleaning advice is generic. The documents that govern your warranty are not. We compared the cleaning sections of [REC's installation manual for its UL-listed panels](https://www.recgroup.com/sites/default/files/2024-11/im_rec_panels_ul_rev_l.pdf), [Qcells' installation manual for the Q.PEAK DUO ML-G9 series](https://media.q-cells.com/v/Sefnn0ti/) (2021 international edition), and [First Solar's module cleaning guidelines](https://www.firstsolar.com/-/media/First-Solar/Technical-Documents/Series-4-Application-Note/Module-Cleaning-Guidelines.ashx), which are written mainly for its large utility-scale modules.

| Topic | REC | Qcells | First Solar |
|---|---|---|---|
| When | Panels cool, e.g. early morning | Cooled panels only; no water if frost is possible | Open circuit or inverter off; dusk to dawn preferred |
| Water | Deionized, at ambient temperature | Lukewarm water | Reverse osmosis best; within 20°C (36°F) of the panel |
| Soap | Mild biodegradable washing-up liquid allowed | No surfactants | Mild detergent, pH 6.5 to 8.5 only |
| Never use | High-pressure hoses; acidic or alkaline detergent | High-pressure water, scrapers, rotating brushes | Ammonia, degreasers, abrasives, over 500 psi |
| Wipe with | Sponge, microfiber cloth, or soft brush | Soft cloth, sponge, or broom; not micro fleece or cotton | Non-conductive nylon brush or sponge |
| Stubborn spots | Isopropyl alcohol under 10% | Isopropyl alcohol within one hour of the stain | Soft cloth and water |
| Standing on panels | Do not stand, walk, lean, or rest buckets | Never step on modules | Cleaning loads kept under 90 lb per module |

First Solar also prohibits cleaners containing hydrochloric acid, D-limonene, or sodium hydroxide, and says never to spray water directly at the junction box, edge seals, or connectors. REC says to rinse with plenty of water and dry with a soft rubber squeegee from the top down, or let the panels air dry.

[Unger, which makes water-fed pole equipment, adds safety rules](https://usa.ungerglobal.com/safety-warning-instructions-for-cleaning-pv-and-solar-systems-with-water-fed-poles/) that apply regardless of panel brand: switch the system off following the manufacturer's procedure, or clean in as little sunlight as possible if you cannot; do not clean if the inverter shows active errors; do not clean from ladders; do not put cold water on very hot panels; and remember that even standing or kneeling on a panel for a short time can cause microcracks.

<a id="where-the-manuals-disagree"></a>
### Where the manuals disagree

Three disagreements are worth knowing before you buy anything:

1. **Dish soap.** REC allows a mild, biodegradable washing-up liquid. Qcells prohibits surfactants, the active ingredient in dish soap. First Solar allows a mild detergent only inside a pH window. The safe default is plain purified water, and soap only if your own manual permits it.
2. **Microfiber.** REC lists a microfiber cloth as a wiping option. Qcells tells owners to use a soft cellulose cloth or sponge and not micro fleece or cotton cloths.
3. **Drying.** REC suggests a soft rubber squeegee or air drying. Unger says not to wipe, sweep, or scrub panels dry after a pure-water rinse. With purified water there is nothing left to squeegee off. With hard tap water, First Solar says to squeegee so scale does not build.

Your installer's paperwork or your monitoring app will name the panel brand. If you cannot find the manual, stick to the rules every source shares: cool panels, low pressure, soft tools, clean water, and no weight on the glass.

<a id="gathering-tools-and-spotting-safety-risks-before-you-climb"></a>
## Gathering Tools and Spotting Safety Risks Before You Climb

Treat the roof as a workplace, not an extension of the backyard. The cleaning kit is simple: a soft brush made for solar glass on a telescoping or water-fed pole, a garden hose with a gentle nozzle, a non-abrasive sponge for isolated spots, and a source of purified or low-mineral water. The [solar panel cleaning brush and pole guide](/blog/solar-panel-cleaning-brush-and-pole/) covers how to choose the pole and brush head. Safe access is the harder part.

<a id="check-the-hazards-before-the-glass"></a>
### Check the hazards before the glass

Hot glass is more than a streaking problem. Midday panels can be hot enough to burn, cold water on hot glass risks thermal shock, and wet glass and wet roof tiles are slick. The wiring behind the array can remain electrically hazardous even when the inverter is off, because the panels keep producing voltage whenever light reaches them.

Before you set up anything, check the entire route from the ground to the array:

- **Access:** If a ladder is unavoidable, [OSHA's portable ladder guidance](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/publications/PORTABLE_LADDER_QC.pdf) calls for a stable, level footing and an extension ladder that reaches at least 3 feet above the roof edge it rests on. A ladder is a way up, not a cleaning platform.
- **Fall protection:** Sloped roofs may require anchors, a harness, and training to use them. A pole will not protect you if you lose your footing.
- **Electrical condition:** Stop if you see cracked glass, damaged cables, loose connectors, or an inverter fault. Do not touch the damaged area.
- **Weather:** Avoid wind, rain, and fast-changing conditions. Santa Ana gusts make a long pole hard to control.
- **Footwear:** Wear soft rubber soles with reliable grip, and never treat the panel surface as a walkway.

Work at dawn or late in the day, when the modules are cool and the roof is dry. If you cannot reach the array safely from the ground or from a properly protected access point, the right tool is a phone, not a longer ladder.

<a id="the-cleaning-process-from-hose-rinse-to-final-rinse"></a>
## The Cleaning Process From Hose Rinse to Final Rinse

1. **Shut down and inspect.** Follow your installer's or manufacturer's shutdown procedure, then look over the array for cracks, loose wiring, and error lights. Do not clean if anything is damaged or the inverter shows a fault.
2. **Wait for cool glass.** Start early in the morning, before the sun heats the panels. That protects the glass from thermal shock and keeps rinse water from drying too fast.
3. **Rinse from the top down.** Use gentle, low-pressure water to float off loose dust, grit, pollen, and ash before a brush touches the glass. Keep the stream away from the junction box, edge seals, and connectors.
4. **Brush in straight passes.** Work a soft brush along the length of each panel, moving dirt toward the lower edge rather than grinding it across the glass. Let the water and bristles do the work, with no downward force.
5. **Final rinse with purified water.** Deionized or reverse osmosis water dries without leaving mineral rings. If you only have tap water, rinse thoroughly and follow your panel manual on squeegeeing before it dries.
6. **Restart and confirm.** Turn the system back on following the same procedure, and check that the inverter returns to normal operation. Investigate any unexpected fault instead of assuming cleaning caused no issue.

<a id="handle-stubborn-deposits-without-forcing-them"></a>
### Handle stubborn deposits without forcing them

Soak bird droppings for a few minutes, then lift them gently with a non-abrasive sponge or soft brush. Do not attack a dry deposit with a scraper, scouring pad, stiff brush, or household degreaser. Those shortcuts scratch glass and damage anti-reflective coatings, and extra pressure does not make the deposit come off any faster. If water alone will not shift it, use only what your panel manual permits, such as REC's dilute isopropyl alcohol.

Sprinkler spots need a different response. They are mineral deposits, so the fix is a purified final rinse and, next time, redirecting the sprinkler head. Pay attention to the frame and lower edge, where calcium-laden runoff collects and can streak the next panel down.

<a id="how-often-to-clean-based-on-where-you-live"></a>
## How Often to Clean Based on Where You Live

A fixed calendar misses the variables that matter most. **Roof pitch, rainfall, salt exposure, dust load, and contamination type** decide how quickly an array needs attention. A steep panel can shed loose debris in rain, while a low-tilt panel keeps a dirty band along its lower edge. REC's manual says the same thing directly: the lower the angle of installation, the more cleaning will be required.

Rain helps, but it is not a complete wash. It moves loose dust and leaves bird deposits, mineral film, and a concentrated dirty edge behind. Watch the production record after unusual weather, then inspect the glass before booking a wash.

| Location profile | Tilt range | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Coastal home with salt exposure | Low to moderate | Inspect monthly; clean when film or deposits return |
| Inland home exposed to Santa Ana dust | Low to moderate | Inspect after wind events; clean when buildup is visible |
| Steep array in a relatively clean setting | Steeper pitch | Often goes longer between washes if inspection stays clear |
| Dusty valley or low-tilt installation | Low or nearly flat | Inspect more often and target the lower edge |

For planning ranges by town, from every 2 to 3 months for near-beach homes to every 6 to 12 months for low-soiling roofs, see our guide on [how often solar panels should be cleaned](/blog/how-often-should-solar-panels-be-cleaned/). Treat those as a starting point and let your production data and the glass confirm the timing.

<a id="when-diy-stops-making-sense-and-a-pro-is-worth-it"></a>
## When DIY Stops Making Sense and a Pro Is Worth It

DIY cleaning makes sense only when access is genuinely safe. A single-story home with a mild roof pitch, stable ground to work from, no damaged components, and panels reachable with a pole may be manageable. Even then, the work takes time, uses water, and gets uncomfortable once the roof heats up.

The calculation changes on a two-story home, a steep roof, or fragile tile. Walking on roof tiles can crack them, and walking on panels can crack cells in ways you cannot see. Both REC and Qcells tell owners not to step on modules at all, and manufacturers can refuse warranty claims for damage caused by cleaning: First Solar's guidelines state that failure to follow them may void the warranty.

<a id="use-clear-stop-signs"></a>
### Use clear stop signs

The following conditions end the DIY discussion:

- **Height:** The array is on a multi-story or steep roof and cannot be reached safely from the ground.
- **Roof material:** Tile, brittle roofing, loose flashing, or signs of water intrusion make foot access risky.
- **Electrical red flags:** You see damaged wiring, cracked glass, exposed connectors, or unexplained inverter faults.
- **Biological growth:** Moss or persistent green growth suggests moisture is collecting beneath or around the array.
- **Personal comfort:** You are straining, rushing, working alone, or using a ladder that is not properly set up. Qcells' manual puts it plainly: never access the installation area alone or without adequate precautions.

A professional crew brings fall-protection procedures, panel-safe brushes, purified water, and experience working around roofing and electrical components. That does not mean every job needs a service visit. It means the price of cleaning should be weighed against the cost of a fall, a cracked tile, a damaged panel, or a warranty dispute. ## Frequently Asked Questions

<details class="faq-item">
<summary>Do you need to turn off solar panels before cleaning?</summary>
<p>Yes, where you can. Unger's safety instructions say to switch the system off following the manufacturer's procedure, and First Solar says to clean only with the inverter off or the modules in open circuit. Switching off does not make the panels dead, though: they produce voltage whenever light reaches them, so clean at dawn or dusk and keep water and tools away from wiring and damaged modules.</p>
</details>

<details class="faq-item">
<summary>What should you not clean solar panels with?</summary>
<p>Pressure washers, scrapers, scouring pads, stiff or rotating brushes, degreasers, and acidic or alkaline cleaners. First Solar also names ammonia, hydrochloric acid, D-limonene, and sodium hydroxide, and caps water pressure at 500 psi. Avoid cold water on hot panels, and never stand or kneel on the glass.</p>
</details>

<details class="faq-item">
<summary>What is the best thing to clean solar panels with?</summary>
<p>Purified water and a soft brush. REC's manual recommends deionized water at ambient temperature with a sponge, microfiber cloth, or soft brush, and First Solar says reverse osmosis water gives the best results. Purified water dries without mineral spots, so nothing needs to be wiped off afterward.</p>
</details>

<details class="faq-item">
<summary>Is Dawn dish soap safe for solar panels?</summary>
<p>It depends on your panel brand. REC allows a mild, biodegradable washing-up liquid, but Qcells prohibits surfactants, the cleaning agents in dish soap, and First Solar only allows mild detergents between pH 6.5 and 8.5. Check the manual for your panels. If you cannot find it, use plain purified water, which is all most light soiling needs.</p>
</details>

<details class="faq-item">
<summary>Can you clean solar panels on a roof without a ladder?</summary>
<p>Often, yes. A water-fed pole with a soft solar brush lets you rinse and brush a single-story array from the ground. If the panels are too high, too far back from the edge, or out of sight from the ground, hire a crew rather than climbing.</p>
</details>

<details class="faq-item">
<summary>Can I walk on the panels?</summary>
<p>No. REC says not to stand or walk on panels, lean on them, or rest buckets on them, and Qcells says never to step on modules. Unger warns that even briefly standing or kneeling on a panel can cause microcracks you cannot see.</p>
</details>

<details class="faq-item">
<summary>Is rainwater enough near the beach?</summary>
<p>Not always. A home close to the coast can keep salt haze, bird deposits, and mineral residue after rain. UC San Diego's California study found that bird droppings in particular do not wash away in rain. Inspect the lower edges and compare production before deciding.</p>
</details>

<details class="faq-item">
<summary>What if I find a crack while cleaning?</summary>
<p>Stop immediately, keep water and tools away from the damaged area, and contact your installer or a qualified solar professional. First Solar warns that cracked modules are a shock hazard and that the risk increases when they are wet.</p>
</details>
